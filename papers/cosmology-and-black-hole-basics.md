# Cosmology and Black Hole Basics for HDC Readers

This short primer is for readers who want the background concepts behind Hierarchical Decoherence Cosmology before reading the main framework paper.

It is not a full textbook treatment. The goal is to explain the minimum ideas needed to understand why HDC talks about black holes, horizons, cosmology, and observer-accessible regions.

## What Is Cosmology?

Cosmology is the branch of physics that studies the large-scale structure and history of the universe.

At its most basic level, cosmology asks questions like:

- How did the observable universe evolve?
- Why is space expanding?
- What happened in the early universe?
- How do gravity, matter, radiation, and quantum physics fit together at cosmic scales?

For HDC, cosmology matters because the framework is not only about small laboratory systems. It asks whether whole regions of the universe, separated by horizons, might relate to one another in a nontrivial quantum-information way.

## What Is the Big Bang?

In standard cosmology, the Big Bang is not usually described as an explosion into empty space. It is the early hot, dense state of the observable universe from which cosmic expansion proceeds.

The key ideas are:

- the early universe was much hotter and denser than it is now,
- space itself has been expanding,
- matter and radiation evolved as the universe cooled,
- and the large-scale universe we observe today developed from that early state.

HDC does not depend on replacing the standard Big Bang picture. But it does sit near questions about how classical spacetime and cosmic history emerge from underlying quantum structure.

## What Is a Black Hole?

A black hole is a region where gravity becomes so strong that, according to general relativity, there is a boundary beyond which signals cannot straightforwardly return to a distant observer.

That boundary is called the event horizon.

For a non-specialist reader, the important point is not every technical detail of black hole geometry. The important point is that black holes create a strong separation between:

- what may happen in an interior region, and
- what an exterior observer can stably access or reconstruct.

That is why black holes are useful in HDC: they make questions about information access and observer dependence much sharper.

## What Is a Horizon?

A horizon is a limit on what information can be straightforwardly accessed by a given observer.

In black hole physics, the horizon is associated with the boundary separating the exterior from the interior. More generally, horizons appear in cosmology whenever there are limits on what parts of spacetime can exchange signals or records with one another.

For HDC, the word horizon matters more than the word black hole by itself. The key question is whether a horizon can prevent the outward propagation of the stable, redundant records needed for an external observer to describe an interior region as one definite classical history.

## Why Do Black Holes Matter for Information?

Black holes became central to modern theoretical physics because they raise a deep tension between gravity, quantum theory, and information.

Very roughly:

- quantum theory strongly suggests information should not simply disappear,
- black holes seem to hide information behind horizons,
- and Hawking radiation made the puzzle more severe by suggesting black holes can evaporate.

That led to the black hole information problem.

HDC does not try to solve that whole problem directly. Instead, it asks a narrower question:

> Even if quantum information is preserved, does that automatically mean a distant observer receives the kind of stable classical records needed to define one externally classicalized history?

HDC's answer is: not obviously.

## How Does This Connect to the Main HDC Idea?

The main HDC proposal is not simply that information can be hidden.

The stronger idea is that an interior region may become effectively classical for internal observers while an external observer still fails to receive the redundant stable records needed to classicalize that region externally.

That is why HDC focuses on distinctions like:

```text
quantum correlations
!= stable records
!= redundant record proliferation
!= externally classicalized history
```

This is where cosmology and black hole physics enter the picture. They provide settings in which horizons may matter not only for what exists, but for what kinds of classical descriptions different observers can legitimately form.
